Bread, butter and a WhatsApp prescription

How one community mental health organisation in Chennai kept care going through the 2020 lockdown. A visual walk through my first research paper, published in the Journal of Public Health.

“Of course, we cannot give bread and butter for all 6000 patients. But we gave provisions for about 300 of them through various sources.” A manager heading outreach at the Schizophrenia Research Foundation, Chennai, on the months after India’s 2020 lockdown

That sentence is from an interview with a service provider at the Schizophrenia Research Foundation (SCARF), a non-government organisation in Chennai that provides care for a range of mental disorders and runs outreach services for rural communities. It is an odd thing to hear from a mental health organisation, because food is not usually counted as part of mental health care. The paper places it in a section on the burden of care at home, where the closure of the vocational centre and day-care came on top of job loss, pay cuts and rising debt for caregivers, and read in that context I find it hard to see where mental health care was supposed to stop and the rest of a family’s troubles begin.

This post is about my first research paper, The COVID-19 pandemic and its impact on mental health services: the provider perspective, published in the Journal of Public Health in October 2021 . It came out of a partnership between LEAD at Krea University and the University of Oxford, funded through the Global Challenges Research Fund’s India–Oxford Initiative, and it asked a fairly simple question: when the country shut down, what happened to community mental health services, and how did the people providing them keep them running?

I should say up front what my part in it was. The interviews, the focus group and the thematic analysis were done by my co-authors. My part was the literature review, which meant reading what had been reported from a geographically and socio-economically spread set of countries and using it to place the Chennai data in a wider context. The journal format is tight, about six pages, and most of the participants’ words had to be cut down to fragments. So, five years on, I wanted to write about it in a form where the quotes get more room and some of the numbers can be drawn out. How the study was done, and what was happening elsewhere at the time, come later in the post; Chennai comes first.

What broke

The first thing to go was access. In the two weeks of complete lockdown, in the paper’s words, “all services were affected”, and even once essential services resumed, patients could not easily reach the centre. Not every neighbourhood pharmacy stocked psychiatric medication, and some pharmacists that did were reluctant to dispense it. In smaller towns, psychiatric care was taken over for COVID-19 treatment. Rural patients were stopped by the police and fined on their way to refill prescriptions at SCARF, even when they showed the prescription.

“These are people who can’t even afford medicines… it felt painful that we were helpless.” A manager heading outreach efforts (P6)

Missed medication meant relapses of conditions like schizophrenia and mood disorders, often needing in-patient care, at exactly the time the centre had stopped admissions for fear of spreading COVID-19 inside the facility. One of the psychiatrists recalled handling “at least ten DESPERATE requests for admissions” (the capitals are in the paper). For patients who had been discharged and rehabilitated, the closure of the vocational centre took away a daily routine as well. The same psychiatrist described the “11 o clock tea” and the people they talked to, and added that most of them keep in touch with the staff regularly: “It was sad for them because this was their social life…” For people living with a chronic mental illness, I suspect that routine and that company were doing some of the work of treatment, even if nobody would have written them into a care plan.

What they built instead

What I find most interesting, reading the paper again, is how ordinary the adaptations were. Prescriptions were written on the official pad, signed, photographed and sent over WhatsApp, in line with the teleconsultation guidelines, and the consultants then spent time on the phone with pharmacists sorting out legibility, brand names, durations and substitutes. A health worker contacted the doctors of clients who were stuck in other districts and passed their prescriptions on. One psychiatrist described calling a pharmacist herself to get an overdue prescription honoured, asking them to dispense “for 1 week or 10 days” until she could see the patient.

That last detail is where my part of the paper meets the data. In the literature I was reviewing, one of the adaptations other health systems had made was to let patients keep using expired prescriptions . The same idea turns up in Chennai, except that here it was not a policy. It was one psychiatrist’s phone call, and it worked as long as she had the time and the pharmacist was willing.

The centre also ran a 24-hour helpline that linked new and existing patients to consultants and therapists. It was open to the public for distress calls, and the government displayed it in its media advertisements, so an NGO’s line became, in effect, part of the public response. The paper notes the cost plainly: existing staff and doctors had their working hours extended to handle the calls. It does not say whether that work was funded, so I will not guess, but it does make me wonder who carries the cost when a public service runs on an organisation’s goodwill.

Some of the infrastructure was even more personal. In the in-patient centres, staff used their own phones to set up video calls with families, partly to settle patients made anxious by the news coverage of COVID-19, and because many patients did not have smartphones. When staff or patients with cards and e-passes were stopped by the police, an administrator would be handed the phone to vouch for them. Decisions, emotional support and the sharing of load all happened in WhatsApp groups. Putting these together (and this is my synthesis, not the paper’s), a good part of the organisation’s continuity ran through its employees’ personal phones.

One of the most concrete changes in clinical practice was time. One psychiatrist described how a first consultation for a new patient, which used to take an hour, was now done in fifteen to twenty minutes.

A natural reading of that number is that care got worse, and I do not think the paper lets us say that. The time with the psychiatrist shrank, but the work was redistributed. A case manager took basic case details on a first phone call, and after the shorter consultation a case worker called the patient back to take the detailed history, which the psychiatrist then read at the next visit. The patient’s story was now held together by a case manager, a psychiatrist, a case worker and the electronic record, rather than by one person in one room. Whether that amounted to a loss, a different kind of care, or in some cases a better use of a psychiatrist’s hour is something interviews with providers alone cannot settle.

What it cost the people carrying it

The paper has a section on the providers’ personal costs, which I think is easy to skim past and should not be, partly because most of those costs follow directly from the adaptations above. The helpline extended working hours. The personal phones never went quiet. An administrative co-ordinator said that the hours after half past four used to be her own, and that she now checked her phone every three to five minutes “because I don’t want to miss anything”. Providers described feeling torn between giving good care and carrying out the new procedures, worried about bringing the virus home to their families, and saw less of their colleagues.

Some of it also cut across the usual hierarchy. The paper notes that responding meant “working across hierarchies”, and that two or three of the respondents held senior management positions on top of their other roles, which goes some way to explaining why a psychiatrist was negotiating with a pharmacist and an administrator was talking to the police.

The paper is also clear about why they kept going: commitment to care “was based on their personal motivation, a perceived sense of fulfilment”, despite the long hours and what it did to their work-life balance. I find that reassuring and a little worrying at once. If an organisation’s resilience rests on the motivation and overtime of its staff, how long can it last when the crisis does not end after two weeks? The study did not measure burnout, so I can only leave that as a question.

Telemedicine, and the room it took away

Telemedicine was the largest single change. The paper’s abstract says it enabled an expansion of both the service and its clientele, as well as efficiency, and the gains were real. It also changed what a therapy session was like, for both sides.

The phrase “casualization” is the one I keep coming back to.The abstract uses “casualization of therapy”; the discussion uses “casualization of the exchange between service provider and patient”. Some clients, as the paper puts it, took phone sessions as “a friendly catch-up call rather than a teletherapy session”. A case worker said that when she asked clients why they had not called, they told her they thought the centre had called “just to ask ‘How are you doing? Are you taking medicine’”. I wonder whether that is partly a side effect of the relationship itself. Years of check-in calls teach people that a call from SCARF is a kind voice asking how they are, and when therapy arrived through the same phone, it is not surprising that some heard it the same way. The work of keeping to a schedule also moved from the client to the counsellor, which the paper counts as an additional burden.

The cost I did not appreciate until I sat with these quotes is privacy. A consulting room is, among other things, a private space, and it is easy not to notice that until it is gone. One outreach manager described families where “women and children have faced a lot of issues”, and asked how a woman could talk about her husband to someone over the phone when the husband was in the room. Moving care into the home moved it, for some women, into the very place where the problem was. The paper makes the general point in its discussion, that privacy issues may matter most “in planning for communities with space constraints”, and I think that one line deserves more weight than its place in the discussion suggests. I should be careful about how far it goes, though: this is one provider’s account of what women could not say, not the women’s own words, and the study cannot tell us how common it was.

Privacy failed at the other end of the line too. The counsellor whose children “would wonder why the person on the other end was crying so much” was describing a cost to herself, and the paper files it that way, under her loss of privacy and family time. I think it was also a cost to the person crying, whose distress was audible to a stranger’s parents and children without anyone having chosen that. The same outreach manager noticed his own position: “I am sitting and talking to you in a sound proof room, but that is not possible for everyone.”In the paper, that remark sits in a passage about public outreach and training moving online, where it was harder to keep up two-way communication with community health workers in rural districts. He goes on: “Women in rural areas were not comfortable with gadgets.” I have used only the first sentence here, as an observation about privacy.

The crowded home that took privacy away also turned family into a resource. Compliance was better where family members became co-therapists, and one patient was rehabilitated with the help of her 10-year-old child. The paper presents that as a success, and it may well have been one. I would only ask, as a question rather than a criticism, what we are asking of a ten-year-old when we make them part of their parent’s treatment.

Relationships, responsiveness, resilience

When the paper pulls these threads together, it names “three crucial elements in continuity and quality service delivery: relationships, responsiveness and resilience” .

Two of the three were in place before the pandemic. The relationships had been built over many years, partly through educating patients and families, and the providers felt that this psycho-education showed its value in how well patients kept to treatment. The resilience drew on lessons from earlier floods, the tsunami and cyclones, and on prior experience with tele-psychiatry, which the paper says made the move online easier. Responsiveness is the one that happened during the crisis, in the continual adaptations described above. I suspect the first two are what made the third possible: provisions can only reach 300 patients if someone already knows who those patients are. The paper does not say which lessons from the earlier disasters carried over, and I would genuinely like to know, because a pandemic is a different kind of disaster, long and everywhere at once, with no unaffected place from which help can arrive.

The view from outside

None of this was unique to Chennai in kind. In October 2020 the World Health Organization published a survey of 130 countries , and the headline figure was blunt.

The literature I was reading at the time was largely about hospitals and health systems. From China there were accounts of psychiatric hospitals reorganising around infection control , and elsewhere descriptions of how inpatient psychiatry and psychiatric care more broadly had been reshaped in the first months . A piece in The Lancet Psychiatry set out how mental health care should change as a consequence . Adaptation was not absent from this work: telecommunication platforms, electronic prescriptions, the continued use of expired prescriptions, isolation and liaison psychiatry were all being reported . What was missing was adaptation in community care run by NGOs, which matters in India because mental health outreach to communities and vulnerable groups here was pioneered by NGOs, and their lessons have proved useful to the public system .

Reading that literature against the Chennai interviews, a few comparisons stand out to me. The expired-prescription policy elsewhere and the psychiatrist’s phone call to a pharmacist are one. Another is the observation that services for rural and older populations, which were always harder to reach, got worse during the pandemic , set against rural patients being fined on their way to collect medicine. A third is the WHO’s split in telemedicine adoption between high- and low-income countries, set against the women in shared homes who could not speak freely on a call. In each case the broad pattern in the literature shows up in Chennai, but at the scale of a single person and a single phone call.

In India specifically, services were disrupted by the nationwide lockdown in March 2020, and the government released guidelines to move to telemedicine . Tele-psychiatry had raised concerns about patient dynamics, proper diagnosis and legal issues , though it had worked in some contexts .

How the study was done

SCARF is, in the paper’s words, a training centre for psychiatry, psychology and psychiatric social work, and a training partner for public mental health services, as well as a provider . That breadth meant one organisation’s staff could speak about many kinds of service, from inpatient wards to rural outreach. The paper also notes the organisation’s prior experience with tele-psychiatry, which probably left it better placed than many.

The people interviewed included managers of clinical services, administrative staff, psychiatrists, social workers, a psychologist and research staff, with between three and more than twenty years at the organisation. Their accounts covered February to December 2020, and the fieldwork itself ran over December 2020 and January 2021. One small thing I should flag: the paper says ten providers were selected and that one interview could not be scheduled, yet quotes are labelled up to P10, so the numbering may not map neatly onto the interviews that took place.

What this study cannot tell us

The paper is direct about its main limitation: “Though limited to a single NGO and to providers, insights from a variety of services within mental healthcare was gleaned” . Both halves of that sentence matter. One organisation based in one city, however broad its services and however far its outreach reaches, is not Indian community mental health care as a whole. And the people interviewed were the people delivering care, not receiving it. We do not know how the shortened consultation felt from the patient’s side, or whether the phone sessions that providers worried were too casual felt that way to the people on the other end.

It is also a retrospective account. The interviews were conducted at the end of 2020 about the months that came before, and what people remember of a crisis, and how they make sense of it afterwards, is not quite the same as what happened at the time. I would rather say that plainly than have the post read as a claim about more than the study can support.

Coming back to it

The paper ends by arguing that these insights are crucial for planning resilient community-based mental health care, and the wider literature at the time was saying something similar: that the pandemic was an opportunity to reform mental health care in India , and that health systems needed to be built with preparedness in mind . The paper is specific about what that would mean: bringing care for people with mental illness into government and city disaster planning, including housing, transport and medication; sensitising the police and other enforcing authorities to their needs, which I read as coming out of the patients fined at checkpoints; training health workers for isolation, infection and relocation; and preparing patients and caregivers for how to manage care during a disaster.

Reading it again now, the question I keep returning to is which of these adaptations outlasted the pandemic, and whether anyone asked patients what the phone intake, the shorter consultation and the WhatsApp prescription changed for them. I do not have an answer, and I suspect it needs the patient and family perspective this study did not have. That includes the families who were not among the 300, and I would like to know what they made of the year.

It was my first time seeing my name on a paper, and I am properly thankful to my co-authors, Sapna Nair, Preethi Kannan, Ananya Raju, John Mathew and Padmavati Ramachandran, for bringing me in, and to the providers at SCARF who gave their time to be interviewed at the end of a very hard year.
