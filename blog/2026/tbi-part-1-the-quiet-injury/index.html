The quiet injury

Part 1 of a series on traumatic brain injury. What a brain injury is, how a blow that never breaks the skull can still damage a brain, why “mild” is a misleading word, and why the injury is better thought of as the start of a long process than as an event.

Somewhere in the world, about once or twice every second, a force from outside the body changes how somebody’s brain works. Most of those people are told the injury is mild, and many walk out of the emergency department with a normal scan. For a good number of them, that is not the end of it.

In 2023, for my undergraduate capstone at Krea University, I spent a few months with a US database of people who had had a traumatic brain injury. I wanted to know which other illnesses tended to turn up together in the years afterwards. The project was supervised by Dr Shyam Kumar Sudhakar. It led to a paper on how common those illnesses were five years after a mild injury , a perspective on why graph theory might help , and a paper on the networks they form . This series explains that work to someone who has never studied medicine, statistics or graph theory, which, at the start, was more or less me.

First, though, I need you to care about the injury. This part covers what happens inside a struck head, why “mild” misleads, and why researchers now treat TBI as a disease that lasts years. Part 2 builds the graph-theory toolkit, and Part 3 puts the two together.

An ordinary injury

By the consensus definition, a traumatic brain injury (TBI) is “an alteration in brain function, or other evidence of brain pathology, caused by an external force” . The force can be a blow, a fall, a sudden stop, a blast or a penetrating object. The result can be anything from a few minutes of confusion to a coma.

How many people does this happen to? Nobody knows to within a factor of two or so. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study estimated 27.08 million new cases in 2016 . The Lancet Neurology Commission on TBI puts it at 50 to 60 million a year, and points out the gap itself . A third estimate, built from different studies, comes to 69 million .

Much of the spread comes from mild injury, which is most injury and often never reaches a hospital. The WHO task force on mild TBI put the hospital-treated rate at about 100 to 300 per 100,000 people a year, and the true rate at “probably above 600/100,000” . More than 90 per cent of all cases are classified as mild . So a number for TBI is mostly a number for mild TBI, and probably an undercount.

One self-reported estimate puts lifetime prevalence at up to 40% of adults . I find that hard to square with how rarely anyone mentions having had one. I suspect much of it is the kind of thing people describe as “I hit my head” and then stop thinking about.

Who gets hurt depends on where people live and how old they are. In high-income countries most TBI comes from falls, “particularly in older people (aged ≥65 years), who often have comorbidities” . In the United States, rates were highest in people aged 75 and over . In low- and middle-income countries road traffic dominates, “particularly those involving motorcyclists” .

India deserves its own paragraph, partly because I live here and partly because the numbers are so thin. The most-cited estimate is still from 2002: “nearly 1.5 to 2 million persons are injured” every year, mostly on the roads . That a twenty-year-old figure is still the reference point is itself a finding. A 2017 review of 72 Indian hospital studies found a mean in-hospital mortality of 24.6%, and judged the quality of reporting low .

What happens after the injury differs just as much. This is the starkest comparison I came across while reading for this series.

Hold on to the second pair: the database my project used is made up entirely of people discharged to inpatient rehabilitation in the United States. In India, that would describe almost nobody.

A soft thing in a hard box

Why can a blow that leaves the skull intact still hurt the brain? Partly because the brain is very soft. Its shear modulus (how easily one layer slides over another) is about 0.4 to 1.4 kilopascals . That is hard to feel, so the usual comparison is soft tofu or a set jelly, which holds its shape on a plate and tears if you pull it.

The brain floats in cerebrospinal fluid, tethered at the bottom by the brainstem and loosely by membranes and blood vessels. The skull is smooth outside, but the inside of its floor is not. The front of the brain rests on the bony roofs of the eye sockets. The tips of the temporal lobes sit in cup-shaped hollows behind a sharp ledge of bone, the sphenoid ridge. As Bigler puts it, these features “are uniquely situated to facilitate injury” to both places . Keep them in mind: the underside of the frontal lobes and the front tips of the temporal lobes.

The model below is a real brain surface, reconstructed from MRI. Turn it, open the skull, and pick a region.

The brain does not divide its work neatly, but roughly, the frontal lobes, especially their underside, are heavily involved in planning, holding back impulses and regulating emotion. The temporal lobes hold the machinery for forming new memories and processing emotion. Both sit on the rough parts of the skull floor. If you were designing a skull to protect personality, mood and memory, you would not put them there.

What a blow does

Now strike the box. The skull moves, and for an instant the loosely held brain stays where it was. The skull’s inner wall catches up and compresses it on the struck side. When the skull stops, the brain carries on and meets the far wall. The bruise at the site of the blow is called a coup injury, and the one opposite a contrecoup injury. Most non-penetrating injury comes from “rapid acceleration or deceleration of the head, with or without impact” . The motion is what hurts the brain, and the skull does not need to break.

Below is a deliberately crude model, a soft body in fluid inside a rigid shell with a ridged floor at the front. It is not a simulation of a brain, but it obeys the same rule: a soft, loosely held thing lags behind its container.

Try the blow to the forehead and the fall backwards. In both, the front of the body hits the front of the shell, once as coup and once as contrecoup. In a real head, each throw forwards or back also scrapes the front of the brain across the ridged floor. That is the intuition for why contusions cluster in the frontal and temporal poles more or less wherever the blow lands .

When the head turns

Surface bruises are only half the story, and for mild injury probably the smaller half. In 1943 the physicist A. H. S. Holbourn argued that brain tissue is very hard to compress but very easy to shear, so rotation damages it more than straight-line motion does . Twist a plate of jelly quickly and the edge goes with the plate while the middle lags, stretching the jelly in between. Ommaya and Gennarelli later proposed that harder rotation shears progressively deeper structures .

The evidence is from monkeys, unpleasant to read but clear. In 1982 Gennarelli and colleagues rotated the heads of 45 primates without any impact, varying only the direction. Front-to-back rotation mostly caused a brief coma, and every concussed animal recovered. Sideways rotation left 20 animals in coma for more than six hours, with the axonal damage seen in severe human injury. The authors concluded that “axonal damage produced by coronal head acceleration is a major cause of prolonged traumatic coma and its sequelae” . Nothing had struck any of those skulls.

How hard is the motion in an ordinary concussion? The best numbers come from American football, where impacts can be filmed, reconstructed or recorded by helmet sensors.

A concussed professional player’s head took, on average, about 98 times the acceleration of gravity for about 15 milliseconds . What I find more telling is the overlap. Concussive impacts in college players ranged from about 61 g to 169 g, and clinical severity had nothing to do with impact size . The same blow can concuss one person and not the next.

What rotation does inside is easiest to picture with the brain’s wiring. Nerve cells talk over long, thin cables called axons, bundled together in the white matter. Each is held in shape by protein tubes (microtubules) that double as transport rails. Axons are “especially vulnerable to injury due to the mechanical loading of the brain”. Diffuse damage to them “may represent a key pathologic substrate of mild TBI (concussion)” .

The stretch lasts milliseconds, but the damage plays out over a day. In people, “axonal degeneration has been found to continue even years after injury” . Nor is it randomly placed. In 434 fatal head injuries, Adams and colleagues graded diffuse axonal injury by how deep it reached. The mildest grade involved the white matter of the hemispheres, the next the corpus callosum (the band joining the two halves), and the worst the upper brainstem .

None of this needs a broken skull, a bleed, or anything visible on the CT scan used in most emergency departments. In one study of 135 people with mild TBI, 27% of those with a normal CT had an abnormal MRI soon after. Some of those MRI findings predicted a worse outcome at three months . A normal CT does not show that the brain was spared.

Some of this damage can be seen in living people, though only on average, by watching water. Inside a bundle of axons, water drifts far more easily along the fibres than across them. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) measures that preference in every small cube of the brain . It summarises it as fractional anisotropy (FA), from 0 if water spreads equally every way to near 1 if only along a line . Following the preferred direction from cube to cube traces the bundles themselves.

The intuition is that a damaged axon should leak, making diffusion less directional and FA lower. In group studies that is broadly what turns up, most consistently in the corpus callosum and the frontal tracts . Then again, the differences are small and shift with time. Some studies in the first two weeks find FA higher than normal, and later ones mostly find it lower . This is also how a CT can be normal while DTI is not. CT shows blood and broken bone, and a stretched axon contains neither.

All of this describes groups, and I want to be plain about that. In the TRACK-TBI study, patients whose CT and MRI were clear showed no significant DTI difference from controls as a group . The radiologists’ review concluded that DTI belongs, for now, to research comparisons, and cannot yet tell whether one particular person’s white matter was injured . The second injury

Everything so far happens in the first fraction of a second. This primary injury is “the direct mechanical damage”, which “cannot be therapeutically influenced” . What follows over hours, days and weeks is the secondary injury, and that is where medicine has tried to intervene.

Giza and Hovda pulled decades of animal work together into what they called the neurometabolic cascade of concussion . It explains why a brain that looks fine can be unusually fragile for a while.

The figure’s stages give the detail. In short, ions flood across damaged membranes, and the cells burn through their energy putting them back. A long slump follows, “a state of impaired metabolism that can last up to 7-10 days in adult animals” .

Everyone who plays a contact sport should know that during this slump, the brain is more vulnerable to a second injury. Clinically, the riskiest window looks like the first ten days. In animals, two injuries three to five days apart did more damage than two injuries seven days apart . Inflammation adds another layer, and it has been linked to later neurodegeneration .

Why there is no drug

If the secondary injury unfolds over days, you would think it could be interrupted, and people have tried for decades. Our BMC Neurology paper says it flatly: “no drug exists to prevent the spread of secondary injuries after a primary impact” . That sentence is backed by an unusual amount of failure.

A 2010 review counted 33 large phase III trials in TBI between 1980 and 2009, with “disappointing results” . Steroids made things worse . Progesterone failed twice: ProTECT III was stopped for futility , and SyNAPSe found no difference . Donald Stein, who had championed progesterone, wrote afterwards that “a 100% failure rate continues to plague the field of TBI trials” . Dr Sudhakar’s own review found that drugs acting on the brain’s main inhibitory signalling system “fail to produce any neuroprotection after a primary impact to the brain” in animal models .

I should be precise, because the claim is easy to overstate. Neurosurgery, intensive care and rehabilitation do help people with TBI. One drug, tranexamic acid, reduced head-injury deaths in mild to moderate TBI when given within three hours, though it works by stopping bleeding rather than by protecting nerve cells . What has not worked, so far, is a drug that protects brain cells from the secondary cascade. Why is argued about, and I am not in a position to settle it. Many of the explanations come back to one point, though, which is that TBI is not one disease. The mix of bruising, bleeding, axonal damage and swelling differs from person to person, and so does what comes after.

Counting consciousness

The most widely used measure of severity is the Glasgow Coma Scale, published by Graham Teasdale and Bryan Jennett in 1974 . It scores how readily a person’s eyes open, how well they talk, and how well they move on command, for a total between 3 and 15. My project and both papers sorted people by the total at admission into mild (13 to 15), moderate (9 to 12) and severe (3 to 8) . Try it.

Play with it and something odd shows up. Eyes opening to speech, confused talk and obeying commands give 3 + 4 + 6 = 13, which is mild. Eyes open spontaneously, orientated talk, but only localising to pressure give 4 + 5 + 5 = 14, also mild, and milder. Very different people land on the same total. Forty years on, Teasdale himself wrote that “individual patients are best described by the three components of the coma scale; whereas the derived total coma score should be used to characterise groups” . That caveat applies to my own work, which used a single total taken at admission.

Mild is not minor

“Mild” describes the score in the first hours, not the consequences. A textbook chapter warns that “for a sizeable minority of patients diagnosed with mTBI, the term can seem a cruel misnomer”. Most people’s symptoms (headache, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, low mood and others) subside within three months. But “in up to 30% of patients with mTBI, they can persist well beyond 6 months” .

Large studies have since found it worse than that. TRACK-TBI followed 1,154 people with mild TBI from eleven US trauma centres, with 299 orthopaedic patients for comparison . Its outcome measure, the Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended, scores 8 for a full return to life as before. Anything lower means some injury-related limitation.

At two weeks, 87% of the mild TBI group reported some limitation. At twelve months 53% still did, against 38% of the orthopaedic patients . The comparison group answers the obvious objection. Any injury bad enough for a trauma centre is disruptive, so some of that 53% has nothing to do with the brain, but the excess is harder to explain away. At three and six months, about a fifth of the mild TBI group had probable post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) or major depression. In the orthopaedic group it was roughly a tenth . In Europe, CENTER-TBI found that 30% of people sent home from the emergency department had not fully recovered at six months .

Yet in CENTER-TBI only 26% were given written information, and 6% a follow-up appointment . The blunt labels cut the other way too. In TRACK-TBI, 52.4% of people with severe TBI and 75% with moderate TBI had a favourable outcome by twelve months . The label given at admission is a rough guide to what follows, in both directions.

A process, not an event

In 2010 Brent Masel and Douglas DeWitt published a paper whose title is the argument: “Traumatic brain injury: a disease process, not an event”. They argued that TBI “increases long-term mortality and reduces life expectancy”, and linked it to illnesses in and beyond the brain. Their aim was not to discourage patients, “but rather to emphasize that TBI should be managed as a chronic disease” . Outcomes can improve or worsen “up to two decades after injury” , and between one and five years, “decline occurs as frequently as improvement” .

The timeline below gathers some of the evidence. It is not one person’s story, and the studies do not follow the same people.

In 2.79 million Danes, any TBI carried about 1.24 times the risk of dementia, rising with the number of injuries . In Denmark, too, the suicide rate was about 1.8 times as high after mild TBI alone . A US study followed 4,351 people with mild TBI and 4,351 matched controls for up to ten years. Mild TBI carried about 2.5 times the risk of high blood pressure, and 5.9 times in people aged 18 to 40. New conditions appeared at a median of three and a half years . In the US database I used, life expectancy was about nine years shorter than in comparable people .

Some of these associations will be causal and some will not. People who fall over, or crash motorbikes, are not a random sample of people. Existing conditions can make an injury more likely, and the same habits or circumstances can raise the risk of both. Studies adjust for what they can, but none can adjust for everything. That applies to my own work too, as Part 3 discusses.

Illnesses that travel together

The word for what that timeline is circling is comorbidity. It means two or more conditions in the same person, together or one after the other. By itself, it says nothing about why. Two neighbouring words do claim a cause. A complication is something the injury or its treatment directly brings about, usually soon after (a seizure in the first week). A sequela is a lasting consequence of the injury (memory problems from a damaged temporal lobe). Comorbidity is the agnostic term, and the honest one for survey data like mine, which shows that two conditions go together but not how.

Why do conditions go together? I found this the most interesting question in the project, and it is the one my data could answer least. There are at least six routes, and they leave very similar footprints.

The first is chance, since two common conditions will often coincide. The second is causation, as when chronic pain leads to depression. The third is a shared mechanism, such as damage to emotional circuits producing both anxiety and low mood. The fourth is a shared risk factor, or confounder; age, for instance, raises the risk of high blood pressure and cataracts together. The fifth is treatment, where a medicine for one condition brings on another. The sixth is detection, because people already under a doctor’s care get examined and diagnosed more. My project could not tell these apart. Knowing they exist, though, changes how you read every line in a comorbidity network.

Here is what is known, and what is suspected, about the conditions that follow a brain injury.

Psychiatric

Anxiety, depression and PTSD were among the five commonest new conditions five years after mild TBI in the database I used . Probable PTSD or major depression was more than twice as common after mild TBI as after an orthopaedic injury at three months . Proposed routes include damage to the regions that regulate emotion and the upheaval any serious injury brings.

Substance use

Here the arrow often points the other way. Alcohol is involved in 15 to 20% of TBIs in India and contributed in a quarter of CENTER-TBI patients , so drinking is as much cause as consequence. Accidental poisoning is among the causes of death with the highest excess risk after TBI .

Heart, blood vessels and metabolism

After mild TBI, high blood pressure, diabetes and stroke all became more likely over the following decade, even in people aged 18 to 40 . Why is not settled. Metabolic dysregulation , reduced activity, poor sleep and stress are proposed routes rather than established ones.

Hormones

The hypothalamus and pituitary, which run most of the body’s hormone systems, sit just above the skull floor. Neuroendocrine dysregulation is a recognised long-term consequence , and injury to that region is the usual proposed route.

Neurological

Seizures, sleep disorders and neurodegenerative disease are all associated with TBI . Continuing axonal degeneration is one proposed link .

Pain and the body

Chronic pain was as common as depression five years after mild TBI , and back pain topped the list ten years after moderate and severe TBI . The accident that injures the brain often injures the body too.

None of these families sits in isolation. Pain disturbs sleep, poor sleep lowers mood, low mood makes it harder to stay active, and inactivity raises blood pressure. Each link is plausible, though nothing here shows that they operate after TBI. Still, a possible circle like that is the best reason I can give for treating these conditions as a network rather than a list.

Tangles

This is where my project begins. The first paper asked a simple question of the TBI Model Systems (TBIMS) database, the largest longitudinal TBI dataset in the world . Five years after a mild TBI, how many people had been diagnosed with each of 26 conditions since their injury ? Of 10,564 people with a recorded admission GCS, 4,915 (46.5%) were mild. One caveat runs through the series: these are not typical concussions. Everyone in TBIMS was admitted for inpatient rehabilitation, which fewer than one in ten hospitalised TBI patients receive . The mild cases typically had visible brain pathology or post-traumatic amnesia . Only 223 to 228 of them answered the questions about other conditions at five years; Part 3 explains why.

Of the 26, ten stand out by how common they were.

Anxiety (16.1%), depression (15.7%), chronic pain (15.7%), high blood pressure (14.0%) and PTSD (11.2%) top the list . Younger people had more depression, and older people more of the conditions that come with age. PTSD was more common in women. The paper concluded that people with mild TBI “could develop serious long-term medical and psychiatric comorbidities which could affect their pace of recovery” . It said nothing about cause, deliberately.

A list of percentages, though, treats each condition as if it lived alone. Someone with depression after a brain injury is also more likely to have anxiety, panic attacks and trouble sleeping. The button above shows that those overlaps are not small, and that once you draw the lines, the list turns into a tangle. My capstone was really about how to see the structure of a tangle like that. Which conditions sit at the centre, and which ones bridge otherwise separate groups? Does the shape change with age? Could a rare condition matter because of where it sits, rather than how often it occurs? And if so, what would a doctor do differently?

Answering those questions needs a way of thinking about connections that most people (and I, at the start of the project) have never been taught. That is what Part 2 is for.
