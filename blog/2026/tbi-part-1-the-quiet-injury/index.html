The quiet injury

Part 1 of a series on traumatic brain injury. What a brain injury is, how a blow that never breaks the skull can still damage a brain, why “mild” is a misleading word, and why the injury is better thought of as the start of a long process than as an event.

Somewhere in the world, about once or twice every second, a force from outside the body changes how somebody’s brain works. Most of those people are told the injury is mild, many walk out of the emergency department with a normal scan, and for a good number of them that is not the end of it.

In 2023, for my undergraduate capstone at Krea University, I spent a few months with a database of people who had been through a traumatic brain injury in the United States, trying to work out which other illnesses tended to turn up together in the years afterwards. The project was supervised by Dr Shyam Kumar Sudhakar, and it led to a paper on how common those illnesses were five years after a mild injury , a perspective piece on why graph theory might help , and a paper that built the illnesses into networks . This series is my attempt to explain that work to someone who has never studied medicine, statistics or graph theory, which, at the start of the project, was more or less me.

Before I can get to networks, though, I need you to care about the injury, and in my experience most people do not think about brain injury at all unless it has happened to someone they know. This first part is about the injury itself: how common it is, what physically happens inside a head when it is struck or shaken, why the word “mild” misleads, and why the people who study it have started to describe it as a disease that goes on for years rather than as an event that is over once the bruise fades. Part 2 builds the graph-theory toolkit from scratch, and Part 3 puts the two together.

An ordinary injury

The consensus definition is broad on purpose. A traumatic brain injury (TBI) is “an alteration in brain function, or other evidence of brain pathology, caused by an external force” . The force can be a blow, the head hitting the ground, a sudden stop in a car, a blast, or a penetrating object, and the alteration can be anything from a few minutes of confusion to a coma. My thesis used a textbook wording that I still like because it spells out what can be affected: damage to the brain from an external mechanical force, “resulting in either temporary or permanent impairment of physical, cognitive, and psychosocial functions, as well as alterations or reductions in consciousness” .

How many people does this happen to? The honest answer is that nobody knows to within a factor of two or so. The Global Burden of Disease study estimated 27.08 million new cases in 2016 . The Lancet Neurology Commission on TBI puts it at 50 to 60 million a year, and points out the gap with the GBD figure itself . A third estimate, built from a different set of studies, comes to 69 million . The Commission also says that “of all common neurological disorders, TBI has the highest incidence” .

Much of the spread comes from the fact that most brain injuries are mild, and much mild injury never reaches a hospital, so any estimate built from hospital records misses a large share of it. The WHO’s task force on mild TBI put the hospital-treated rate at about 100 to 300 per 100,000 people a year, and the true population rate at “probably above 600/100,000” . A Canadian study of concussion found 1,153 per 100,000 per year . Between 70 and 90 per cent of treated brain injuries are mild , and the Commission puts it at more than 90 per cent of all cases . So when you read a number for TBI, it is mostly a number for mild TBI, and it is probably an undercount.

The cost is easier to pin down than the count. The Commission puts it at around US$400 billion a year to the global economy, and in Europe alone more than two million people are admitted to hospital with TBI each year and about 82,000 die . In the United States in 2013 there were about 2.8 million TBI-related emergency visits, hospitalisations and deaths . One self-reported estimate quoted in the textbook my thesis leaned on has lifetime prevalence at up to 40% of adults , which I find hard to square with how rarely anyone mentions having had one. I suspect that is because a large part of it is the kind of thing people describe as “I hit my head” and then stop thinking about.

Who, how and where

Who gets hurt, and how, depends a great deal on where they live and how old they are. In high-income countries most TBI comes from falls, “particularly in older people (aged ≥65 years), who often have comorbidities” . In the United States the highest rates of TBI-related emergency visits, hospitalisations and deaths were in people aged 75 and over (2,232.2 per 100,000), followed by the very young (0 to 4) and people aged 15 to 24, and falls in the over-75s drove much of the rise in emergency visits between 2007 and 2013 . In low- and middle-income countries the picture is driven by road traffic, “particularly those involving motorcyclists” . Globally, falls were 52% of causes in 1990 and 54% in 2019 .

India deserves its own paragraph, partly because I live here and partly because the numbers are so thin. The estimate that is still cited most often is from 2002: “nearly 1.5 to 2 million persons are injured” every year, with road traffic the leading cause at about 60%, followed by falls at 20 to 25% and violence at about 10%, and alcohol involved in 15 to 20% of cases . That a twenty-year-old figure is still the reference point is itself a finding. A 2017 systematic review pooled 72 studies of 19,962 patients from 14 states over 27 years and found a mean age of 31.3, a male to female ratio of 3.8 to 1, and a mean in-hospital mortality of 24.6%; it also noted that “over a quarter of the world’s trauma deaths occur in India, with traumatic brain injury (TBI) as the leading cause of death and disability within trauma”, and that the quality of reporting was low . Road traffic accounted for 55.5% of the injuries in that review and falls for 29.2% .

What happens after the injury differs just as much. A comparison in the Commission’s report is the starkest pair of numbers I came across while reading for this series.

Hold on to that second pair. The database my project used is made up entirely of people who were discharged to inpatient rehabilitation in the United States, which, in India, would describe almost nobody.

A soft thing in a hard box

To see why a blow that leaves the skull intact can still hurt the brain, it helps to know what kind of object the brain is, physically. It is very soft. When engineers press on slices of brain to measure how stiff it is, they find moduli of about 1.4 kilopascals for grey matter and 1.9 for white matter in cow brain , and in human tissue the best-fitting models give a shear modulus (how easily it deforms when one layer slides over another) of about 0.4 to 1.4 kilopascals . Those numbers are hard to feel, so here is an image rather than a measurement: the comparison usually made is to soft tofu or a set jelly. It holds its shape on a plate, and it tears if you pull on it. Mechanically, it is “nonlinear and viscoelastic” , which means it behaves partly like a spring and partly like honey, and stiffens the more you deform it.

That soft object does not sit directly against bone. It floats in cerebrospinal fluid, a thin layer of clear liquid that fills the space between brain and skull, and it is tethered at the bottom by the brainstem and loosely by membranes and blood vessels. The skull around it is hard and, on the outside, smooth. The inside of its floor is not. The front of the brain rests on the bony roofs of the eye sockets, and the tips of the temporal lobes sit in two cup-shaped hollows on either side, behind a sharp ledge of bone called the sphenoid ridge. As Bigler puts it, “these concavities of the skull base cup the frontal and temporal lobes”, and the ridge and the edge of the tentorium (a stiff fold of membrane that separates the back of the brain from the cerebellum) “are uniquely situated to facilitate injury to the posterior base of the frontal lobe and the anterior pole and medial surface area of the temporal lobe” . Keep those two places in mind: the underside of the frontal lobes and the front tips of the temporal lobes.

The model below is a real brain surface, reconstructed from MRI, inside a skull. Turn it, open the skull, and pick a region to see where it is and what it does.

The functions in that model are summaries, and the brain does not really divide its work this neatly, but the broad strokes matter for this series. The frontal lobes, and in particular their underside (the orbitofrontal cortex), are heavily involved in planning, holding back impulses and regulating emotion. The temporal lobes hold the machinery for forming new memories and for processing emotion. These are the regions that sit on the rough parts of the skull floor. If you were designing a skull to protect personality, mood and memory, you would not put them there.

What a blow does

Now strike the box. The skull is pushed one way, and for a brief moment the brain, which is only loosely attached and has inertia of its own, stays where it was. The skull’s inner wall catches up with the brain on the side that was struck and compresses it there, and when the skull is stopped (by the neck, or by the floor) the brain carries on moving and meets the far wall. Neuropathologists call the bruise at the site of the blow a coup injury and the bruise on the opposite side a contrecoup injury. The textbook chapter on neuropathology describes the mechanism of most non-penetrating injury as “rapid acceleration or deceleration of the head, with or without impact” . “With or without impact” is the important part. What hurts the brain is the motion, and the skull does not need to break for that to happen.

Below is a deliberately crude model of the idea: a soft body in a fluid inside a rigid shell, with a ridged floor at the front. It is not a brain and not a simulation of one, but it obeys the same basic rule, which is that a soft, loosely held thing lags behind its container.

Try the blow to the forehead and the fall backwards a few times each. In both, the front of the body ends up hitting the front of the shell, once as the coup and once as the contrecoup. In a real head the front of the brain also rests on the ridged floor, so every time it is thrown forwards or back it scrapes across bone as well. That is the intuition behind a pattern that surprises most people, which is that contusions cluster in the frontal and temporal poles more or less regardless of where on the head the blow lands . The skull floor is shaped so that whatever the brain does in those first milliseconds, its front and its temporal tips grind against something rough.

When the head turns

Bruises on the surface are only half of the story, and for mild injury probably the smaller half. In 1943 the physicist A. H. S. Holbourn, reasoning from physical models of the head, argued that brain tissue is very hard to compress but very easy to shear, and that it is therefore rotation of the head, more than straight-line acceleration, that damages it . Think of the jelly again. Push it straight and it moves as a block; twist the plate quickly and the edge goes with the plate while the middle lags behind, and the jelly in between is stretched. Ommaya and Gennarelli later tied this idea to concussion and loss of consciousness, proposing that as the severity of rotation rises, the shearing reaches progressively deeper structures .

The experimental evidence for this is from monkeys, and it is not pleasant to read, but it is clear. In 1982 Gennarelli and colleagues accelerated the heads of 45 primates without any impact at all, varying only the direction of the rotation . Brief coma of under 15 minutes (a concussion) followed in 11 of 13 animals rotated front to back, 2 of 6 rotated obliquely and 2 of 26 rotated sideways. All 15 concussed animals recovered and showed no diffuse axonal injury. But 20 of the animals rotated sideways were in coma for more than six hours, all were severely disabled, and their brains showed the axonal damage seen in severe human injury. The authors concluded that “axonal damage produced by coronal head acceleration is a major cause of prolonged traumatic coma and its sequelae” , and nothing had struck any of those skulls.

How hard is the motion in an ordinary concussion? The best numbers come from American football, because players can be filmed, reconstructed with crash-test dummies or fitted with sensors in their helmets.

A concussed professional player’s head, on average, briefly experienced about 98 times the acceleration of gravity, over about 15 milliseconds . What I find more telling is how much the ranges overlap. In the college study, concussive impacts ranged from about 61 g to about 169 g, and how severe the concussion was clinically had nothing to do with the size of the impact. The authors were candid that “it may be difficult to establish a threshold for concussive injury that can be applied to all football players” . The same blow can concuss one person and not the next, which is part of why concussion is so hard to see from the outside.

What the rotation does inside is easiest to picture with the brain’s wiring. Nerve cells talk over long, thin cables called axons, bundled together in the white matter, and each axon is held in shape by an internal scaffold of protein tubes (microtubules) that also act as the rails along which the cell transports material. Axons are, in the words of one review, “especially vulnerable to injury due to the mechanical loading of the brain”, and diffuse axonal injury “has been found in all severities of TBI and may represent a key pathologic substrate of mild TBI (concussion)” .

In other words, the stretch lasts milliseconds but the damage plays out over a day, and in people, “axonal degeneration has been found to continue even years after injury” . Where it happens is also not random. In a classic study of 434 fatal non-penetrating head injuries, Adams and colleagues found diffuse axonal injury in 122, and graded it by where the damage reached: the white matter of the hemispheres, then a focal lesion in the corpus callosum (the thick band of fibres joining the two halves of the brain), then a lesion in the upper brainstem . In 24 of those cases the injury could only be seen under the microscope. The last model below shows the same idea in three dimensions, with the long fibre bundles drawn in.

None of the damage in this section needs a broken skull, a bleed, or anything that shows up on the CT scan used in most emergency departments. In one study of 135 people with mild TBI, 27% of those with a normal CT on admission had an abnormal MRI soon after, and contusions or four or more small bleeds along axons on that MRI predicted a worse outcome at three months . A normal CT, on its own, does not show that the brain was spared.

There is a way to see some of this damage in living people, though only on average, and it relies on water. Water molecules in the brain are always jiggling about at random, and in most tissue they wander as easily in one direction as another. Inside a bundle of axons that changes, because the membranes and the myelin wrapped around them make it far easier for water to drift along the fibres than across them. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) measures that preference in every small cube of the brain , and summarises it as a number between 0 and 1 called fractional anisotropy, or FA: 0 if water spreads equally in every direction, and close to 1 if it spreads only along a line . By following the preferred direction from one cube to the next, you can trace the long bundles themselves. The figure below shows tracts traced this way through an average of 842 brains.

The intuition is that a damaged axon should leak, which would make diffusion less directional and FA lower, and in group studies that is broadly what turns up. A meta-analysis of thirteen studies found lower FA in the corpus callosum of people with mild TBI than in controls, most clearly in its back end, the splenium . A tally of the wider literature found the frontal tracts reported most often, the corona radiata and the front of the corpus callosum among them . Then again, the differences are small, and they shift with time: some studies in the first two weeks find FA higher than normal, and later studies mostly find it lower . This is also how a CT can be normal while DTI is not, because CT is very good at showing blood and broken bone, and a stretched axon contains neither.

All of this describes groups, and I want to be plain about that. In the TRACK-TBI study, patients whose CT and MRI were clear showed no significant DTI difference from controls as a group , and the radiologists’ review of the evidence concluded that DTI belongs, for now, to research comparisons, and cannot yet tell whether one particular person’s white matter was injured . What the figure can do is show where in the wiring group studies have looked and found differences.

The second injury

So far everything I have described happens in the first fraction of a second. The neuroscientists who study concussion call this the primary injury, and it is, in one review’s words, “the direct mechanical damage”, which “cannot be therapeutically influenced” . By the time anyone could treat it, it is over. What follows over the next hours, days and weeks is the secondary injury, and that is where medicine has tried to intervene. The textbook chapter on epidemiology puts it bluntly: “there is no effective treatment to reverse the effects of the primary brain injury sustained, and treatment is aimed at minimising the secondary brain injury that can occur due to the effects of ischaemia, hypoxia and raised intracranial pressure” .

Giza and Hovda pulled together decades of animal work into what they called the neurometabolic cascade of concussion . It is worth walking through slowly, because it explains why a brain that looks fine can be, for a while, unusually fragile.

The sequence, roughly, is this. The mechanical stretch pokes holes in cell membranes, so that potassium floods out of nerve cells and sodium and calcium flood in, and the cells release glutamate, a signalling chemical, “hyperacute” and “indiscriminate” . Every cell then tries to pump the ions back where they belong, and the pumps run on energy, so “ATP-requiring membrane ionic pumps shift into overdrive, causing hyperglycolysis” (a burst of glucose burning) and a “relative depletion of intracellular energy reserves” . The calcium that got in is mopped up by mitochondria, the cell’s power stations, which impairs them. After the burst comes a long slump: glucose metabolism falls into “a state of impaired metabolism that can last up to 7-10 days in adult animals” . Calcium inside axons also collapses their internal scaffolding, which links the cascade back to the axon figure above.

There is a practical consequence that I think everyone who plays a contact sport should know about. During that slump the brain is more vulnerable to a second injury. Clinically the window of greatest vulnerability looks like the first ten days, and in animal models two injuries three to five days apart did more damage than two injuries seven days apart . Inflammation adds another layer over the following days and weeks, and it has been linked to later neurodegeneration .

Why there is no drug

If the secondary injury unfolds over days, you would think it should be possible to interrupt it. People have been trying for a long time. The abstract of our BMC Neurology paper says it flatly: “no drug exists to prevent the spread of secondary injuries after a primary impact” . That sentence is backed by an unusual amount of failure.

A review of the field in 2010 counted 33 large phase III trials in TBI between 1980 and 2009, and said that their “disappointing results … have led to a critical reappraisal of clinical trial methodology” . Steroids, which reduce swelling elsewhere in the body, made things worse: in the CRASH trial of more than ten thousand adults, more people died within two weeks on steroids than on placebo . Progesterone failed in two large trials. ProTECT III was stopped for futility after 882 of a planned 1,140 patients , and in SyNAPSe the progesterone and placebo groups had favourable outcomes 50.4% and 50.5% of the time . Donald Stein, who had championed progesterone, wrote afterwards that “a 100% failure rate continues to plague the field of TBI trials” . A systematic review of 191 completed trials of acute care concluded that “considerable investment of resources … has resulted in very little translatable evidence” . Anti-inflammatory drugs have also failed in trials , and Dr Sudhakar’s own review of drugs that act on the brain’s main inhibitory signalling system found that in animal models they “fail to produce any neuroprotection after a primary impact to the brain” and can be harmful .

I should be precise about the claim, because it is easy to overstate. There are treatments that help people with TBI: neurosurgery, intensive care that keeps oxygen and blood pressure up and pressure inside the skull down, and rehabilitation. And one drug did reduce deaths in a large trial. Tranexamic acid, which stops bleeding rather than protecting nerve cells, reduced head-injury deaths in people with mild to moderate TBI when given within three hours, though not in severe TBI . What has not worked, so far, is a drug that protects the brain’s cells from the secondary cascade itself. Why is a question the field argues about, and I am not in a position to settle it. Many of the explanations offered come back to the same thing, though, which is that TBI is not one disease: the mix of bruising, bleeding, axonal damage and swelling differs from one person to the next, and so does what comes after. Which brings me to how we decide who has a mild injury and who has a severe one.

Counting consciousness

The most widely used measure of how badly a brain is injured is the Glasgow Coma Scale, published by Graham Teasdale and Bryan Jennett in 1974 . It asks three questions of a person who may not be able to answer you, and scores each: how readily do their eyes open, how well can they talk, and how well can they move on command. The three scores add up to a total between 3 and 15. My project, and both of the papers, describe sorting people by the total score recorded at admission into mild (13 to 15), moderate (9 to 12) and severe (3 to 8) . Try it.

Play with it for a minute and something odd shows up. A person who opens their eyes only when spoken to, is confused, and obeys commands scores 3 + 4 + 6 = 13: mild. A person who opens their eyes spontaneously, is orientated, but can only localise to pressure instead of obeying scores 4 + 5 + 5 = 14: also mild, and milder. Very different people can land on the same total. Teasdale himself, revisiting the scale forty years on, wrote that “individual patients are best described by the three components of the coma scale; whereas the derived total coma score should be used to characterise groups”, and that predicting outcome needs other information as well . That is a caveat that applies to my own work, which sorted people by a single total taken at admission.

Mild is not minor

Here is the part of the story that I most want readers to take away. “Mild” in “mild TBI” describes the score in the first hours, not the consequences. The textbook chapter on mild injury says that “for a sizeable minority of patients diagnosed with mTBI, the term can seem a cruel misnomer”, and that although most people’s symptoms (fatigue, irritability, poor concentration, headache, dizziness, sensitivity to noise and light, depression and anxiety) subside within three months, “in up to 30% of patients with mTBI, they can persist well beyond 6 months” .

Large studies since then have found it to be worse than that. TRACK-TBI followed 1,154 people with mild TBI from eleven US trauma centres, alongside 299 people with orthopaedic injuries (a broken leg, say) as a comparison . The outcome measure is the Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended, whose top score (8) means a full return to the life a person had before; anything below 8 means some injury-related limitation, at work, at home or socially.

At two weeks, 87% of the people with mild TBI in TRACK-TBI reported some functional limitation. At twelve months it was still 53%, against 38% of the orthopaedic patients, and the authors concluded that “most patients with mTBI presenting to US level I trauma centers report persistent, injury-related life difficulties at 1 year postinjury” . That comparison group matters, because it answers the obvious objection: being injured badly enough to go to a trauma centre is disruptive whatever the injury, and some of that 53% would be there for reasons unrelated to the brain. The excess over the orthopaedic group is the part that is harder to explain away. The same study found that at three and six months about a fifth of the mild TBI group had probable post-traumatic stress disorder or major depression, against roughly a tenth of the orthopaedic group . In Europe, CENTER-TBI found that 30% of people sent home from the emergency department had not fully recovered at six months , and the Commission summarises the European and American studies together as showing that “the persisting effects of mild TBI are common and well outside the conventional perception of mild” .

Those are the people most likely to be told they are fine and sent home, and the Commission’s report notes how little follows: in CENTER-TBI only 26% were given written information and 6% a follow-up appointment, and 90% of centres do not routinely schedule follow-up after discharge from the emergency department . The papers my project produced quote an old description of mild TBI as a “silent epidemic”, “because the sign and symptoms of the injury don’t usually manifest immediately leading to difficulties in diagnosis” . The other side of the blunt labels is worth stating too: in TRACK-TBI, by twelve months 52.4% of people with severe TBI and 75% of those with moderate TBI had a favourable outcome . The severity label given at admission is a rough guide to what follows, in both directions.

A process, not an event

In 2010 Brent Masel and Douglas DeWitt published a paper whose title is the argument: “Traumatic brain injury: a disease process, not an event” . They argued that TBI “is a chronic disease process, one that fits the World Health Organization definition”, that it “increases long-term mortality and reduces life expectancy”, and that it is associated with seizures, sleep disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, hormonal problems and psychiatric illness, as well as conditions well outside the brain such as metabolic dysregulation. They were careful to add that their “intent is not to discourage patients with TBI or their families and caregivers, but rather to emphasize that TBI should be managed as a chronic disease” . Corrigan and Hammond made the same case from rehabilitation, describing brain injuries as “dynamic conditions that continue to change years after onset” , and a Lancet Neurology review found that outcomes can improve or worsen “up to two decades after injury” . Between one and five years after injury, according to the Commission, “decline occurs as frequently as improvement” .

A few of these deserve a sentence each. In a study of 2.79 million Danes, people with any TBI had about 1.24 times the risk of dementia of people without one, rising with the number of injuries, and the excess was largest in the first six months . In 7.4 million Danes, the suicide rate was about twice as high after TBI, and still about 1.8 times as high after mild TBI alone . And in a US study that matched 4,351 people with mild TBI to 4,351 unexposed people and followed them for up to ten years, mild TBI was associated with about 2.5 times the risk of developing high blood pressure, 1.9 times the risk of diabetes and 2.2 times the risk of ischaemic stroke or transient ischaemic attack (a “mini-stroke”), with new conditions appearing at a median of three and a half years after injury . In people aged 18 to 40 with mild TBI, the hazard ratio for high blood pressure was 5.9. The authors called for “a targeted screening program for multisystem diseases after TBI” . For the people in the US database I used, who had more severe injuries on average, life expectancy was about nine years shorter than for comparable people in the general population .

Some of these associations will be causal and some will not. People who fall over are not a random sample of people, and neither are people who crash motorbikes; conditions that were already there can make an injury more likely, and the same habits or circumstances can raise the risk of both. Most of these studies adjust for what they can, and none of them can adjust for everything. I come back to this in Part 3, because it applies just as much to my own work.

Illnesses that travel together

The word for what that timeline is circling is comorbidity, and since the rest of the series hangs on it, I want to pin it down. Comorbidity means two or more conditions in the same person, whether at the same time or one after the other. It says nothing, by itself, about why. Two neighbouring words do carry a claim about cause, and it helps to keep them apart. A complication is something the injury or its treatment directly brings about, usually soon after (a seizure in the first week, an infection in hospital). A sequela is a lasting consequence of the injury (memory problems from a damaged temporal lobe). Comorbidity is the broader, more agnostic term, and it is the honest one for the kind of data my project used, because a survey that records which conditions a person has can tell you that two things go together without telling you how.

So why do conditions go together? This was the question I found most interesting in the project, and also the one my data could answer least. There are at least six routes, and they leave very similar footprints in data.

The first route is plain chance. If high blood pressure affects a lot of older people and so do cataracts, plenty of people will have both without either having anything to do with the other, and a common condition will turn up alongside almost everything. The second is the one people usually assume: one condition causes the other, as when chronic pain wears a person down into depression. The third is a shared mechanism, a single underlying process that produces both, as when the same damage to frontal and limbic circuits could make someone both more anxious and more prone to low mood. The fourth is a shared risk factor, sometimes called a confounder: age raises the risk of high blood pressure, high cholesterol, cataracts and osteoarthritis all at once, and alcohol, poverty or a dangerous job can do the same for other sets of conditions. The fifth is treatment, where a medicine for one condition brings on another. The sixth is detection: a person who is already under a doctor’s care for one condition gets examined, asked about and diagnosed more, so conditions look more connected among patients than they are in life. My project could not tell these apart, and I say so again in Part 3, but knowing they exist changes how you read every line in a comorbidity network.

With that in hand, here is what is known, and what is suspected, about the conditions that follow a brain injury, family by family.

Psychiatric

Anxiety, depression and post-traumatic stress disorder were among the five commonest new conditions five years after a mild TBI in the database I used, with panic attacks not far behind , and probable PTSD or major depression was more than twice as common after mild TBI as after an orthopaedic injury at three months, and still more common at six . Two routes are usually proposed and probably both operate: damage to the frontal and temporal regions that regulate emotion , and the disruption to work, money and relationships that any serious injury brings.

Substance use

Here the arrow often points the other way. Alcohol is involved in 15 to 20% of TBIs in India and was a contributing factor for a quarter of patients in CENTER-TBI , so drinking is as much a cause of injury as a consequence, which is the shared-risk-factor route in action. Accidental poisoning is among the causes of death with the highest excess risk after TBI .

Heart, blood vessels and metabolism

After mild TBI, high blood pressure, diabetes and stroke (or transient ischaemic attack) all became more likely over the following decade, even in people aged 18 to 40 . Why is not settled. Masel and DeWitt list neuroendocrine and systemic metabolic dysregulation among TBI’s long-term effects ; reduced activity, sleep problems and stress are other plausible contributors, and I would treat all of these as proposed routes rather than established ones.

Hormones

The hypothalamus and pituitary, which run most of the body’s hormone systems, sit at the base of the brain just above the skull floor described earlier, and neuroendocrine dysregulation is one of the long-term consequences Masel and DeWitt name . Injury to that region is the usual proposed route.

Neurological

Seizures, sleep disorders and neurodegenerative disease are all associated with TBI , and TBI is a risk factor for epilepsy, stroke and neurodegeneration . The dementia risk rises with the number of injuries , and continuing axonal degeneration is one proposed link .

Pain and the body

Chronic pain was as common as depression five years after mild TBI , and back pain topped the list ten years after moderate and severe TBI . The accident that injures the brain often injures the rest of the body too, which is the most direct route of all, and age adds osteoarthritis on top.

None of these families sits in isolation, and it is easy to imagine how they might feed one another: pain disturbing sleep, poor sleep lowering mood, low mood making it harder to stay active, inactivity raising blood pressure, and so on around a circle. Each of those links is plausible, and some are well studied in other settings, but nothing in this post shows that they operate after TBI. Still, the possibility of a circle like that is the best reason I can give for looking at these conditions as a network rather than as a list.

Tangles

This is where my project begins. The first paper that came out of it asked a simple question of the TBI Model Systems (TBIMS) database, which is the largest longitudinal TBI dataset in the world and has followed some participants for 35 years : five years after a mild TBI, how many people have been diagnosed with each of 26 medical and psychiatric conditions since their injury ? Of the 10,564 people in the database whose admission GCS was recorded, the paper counted 4,915 (46.5%) in the mild range. A caveat that runs through the whole series is that these are not typical concussions. Everyone in TBIMS was admitted for inpatient rehabilitation, which fewer than one in ten hospitalised TBI patients receives , and the mild cases there were “typically characterized by the presence of intracranial pathology and/or PTA” (post-traumatic amnesia, a stretch of time after the injury the person cannot remember) . Only 223 to 228 of them answered the questions about other conditions at the five-year follow-up, for a reason Part 3 explains.

Of the 26, ten stand out by how common they were.

Anxiety (16.1%), depression (15.7%), chronic pain (15.7%), high blood pressure (14.0%) and post-traumatic stress disorder (11.2%) top the list . The younger people (50 or under at the follow-up) had more depression than the older ones, and the older ones more high blood pressure, high cholesterol, cataracts and osteoarthritis, which is roughly what you would expect from age alone; post-traumatic stress disorder was more common in women. The paper concluded that “individuals who have sustained mild TBI could develop serious long-term medical and psychiatric comorbidities which could affect their pace of recovery” , and it said nothing about cause, deliberately.

A list of percentages, though, treats each condition as if it lived alone, and the previous section was about why that is wrong. Someone with depression after a brain injury is also more likely to have anxiety, panic attacks and trouble sleeping, and the press of a button above shows that those overlaps are not small. Once you start drawing the lines, the list turns into a tangle, and the question my capstone was really about is how you see the structure of a tangle like that. Which conditions sit at the centre, which ones bridge otherwise separate groups, and does the shape of the tangle change with age? Could a condition that is rare be important because of where it sits, rather than how often it occurs? And if it could, what would a doctor do differently with that knowledge?

Answering those questions needs a way of thinking about connections that most people (and I, at the start of the project) have never been taught. That is what Part 2 is for.
