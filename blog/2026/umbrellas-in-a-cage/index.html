Umbrellas in a cage

Some small molecules turn themselves inside out like an umbrella in the wind, and quantum mechanics lets them do it through a wall of energy they cannot climb. A visual walk through my paper on what happens to that flip when the molecule is shut inside a C₆₀ football, and an introduction to how chemists calculate any of this, density functional theory included.

Coming back to it
A molecule of ammonia, started in one shape, tunnels inside out and back about twenty-four billion times a second, through an energy barrier it does not have enough energy to climb. This post is about two of its cousins, and about what happens to that flip when you lock the molecule inside a cage of sixty carbon atoms.

The paper this post is about asks a narrow question: if you take a small, pyramid-shaped molecule that likes to flip inside out, and shut it inside a C₆₀ fullerene, does the flip get easier or harder, and why? We looked at two molecules, the hydronium ion (OH₃⁺, the thing that makes acids acidic) and phosphine (PH₃), and the answer turned out to be different for each . The work was done at Krea University with Dr Brijesh Kumar Mishra and Dr Sivakumar Srinivasan, together with Shreya Chidambaram and Netra Krishna.

I came to this project without having done any quantum chemistry. I had to teach myself how to use ORCA, what a basis set was, why there were dozens of “functionals” with names like B3LYP and M06-2X, and how to keep a cluster busy without breaking it. So this post is written for someone in roughly the position I was in: you have done some physics and chemistry, you have heard the words “quantum tunnelling” and “DFT”, and you would like to know what they actually mean. The first third builds up the idea of inversion and tunnelling, the middle third is a tour of how molecules are calculated, and the last third puts the two together to read the paper’s results. The practical side, how I learned ORCA and ran these jobs on a cluster, will get a post of its own.

An umbrella in the wind

Ammonia, NH₃, is shaped like a short, squat pyramid: a nitrogen atom on top, three hydrogens forming the base. The nitrogen also carries a pair of electrons that are not shared with anything, a “lone pair”, which sits on the side away from the hydrogens and pushes them down, a bit like the canopy of an umbrella with the handle being the lone pair.

Now imagine the umbrella in a strong wind. The canopy goes flat, and then pops through to the other side. A pyramidal molecule can do the same thing: the central atom passes through the plane of the three hydrogens and comes out on the other side, and you end up with the same pyramid, pointing the other way. This is called umbrella inversion, and the halfway point, where the molecule is completely flat, is the hardest part of the journey.

The two molecules in the paper make a useful pair. Hydronium is a water molecule that has picked up an extra proton. It is positively charged, it is flatter than ammonia (the H–O–H angle is about 112°, against about 107° in ammonia), and it flips easily. Phosphine is ammonia with the nitrogen swapped for phosphorus, the next element down the same column of the periodic table. Its bonds are longer, and its H–P–H angle is only about 93°, so it is a tall, narrow pyramid, and it almost never flips. The reason the angles differ so much is a story about how much the phosphorus 3s and 3p orbitals mix, which I will leave aside here; what matters for us is that one of these molecules sits near the easy end of inversion, and the other near the hard end.

Two valleys and a hill

To say anything quantitative, we need to talk about energy. Picture a line along which the molecule flips, from “pyramid pointing up”, through “flat”, to “pyramid pointing down”, and plot the molecule’s energy at each point along it. The two pyramids are equally comfortable, so they sit at the bottoms of two identical valleys. The flat shape is strained, so it sits at the top of a hill between them. Chemists call this a double-well potential, the top of the hill is the transition state, and the height of the hill above the valley floor is the inversion barrier.

The horizontal axis needs a name, and the paper calls it the reaction coordinate. Informally, it measures how far the molecule is from flat, and which way it is pointing. (Formally, it is a “mass-weighted” distance, measured in units of bohr·amu½, which takes into account that it is mostly the light hydrogens that move. You can ignore the units and think of it as “umbrella-ness”.)

A detour on units

Spectroscopists measure energies in wavenumbers, cm⁻¹, which looks strange for an energy. The idea is that light of a given energy has a given wavelength, and the wavenumber is just how many of those wavelengths fit into a centimetre, so higher wavenumber means higher energy. Some conversions to hold on to: 1 kcal/mol is about 350 cm⁻¹, and the typical thermal energy available to a molecule at room temperature, kBT, is about 208 cm⁻¹.

On that scale, the hydronium barrier of about 700 cm⁻¹ is only about three and a half times the thermal energy, and the phosphine barrier of about 11,600 cm⁻¹ is more than fifty times it.

If molecules behaved like balls rolling on a landscape, the story would end here. A ball with less energy than the hill would roll back and forth inside one valley forever. A ball with more would roll over the top. The barrier would be a wall, and only molecules that were hot enough would ever flip.

Going through the hill

Molecules are not balls, though, and at this scale the rules are quantum mechanical. Two things change.

The first is that a molecule cannot have any energy it likes. Confined to a valley, it can only have certain energies, the energy levels, and it can never sit perfectly still at the bottom: even the lowest level sits some way above the valley floor (this leftover energy is called the zero-point energy). Each level comes with a wavefunction, ψ, whose square tells you how likely you are to find the molecule at each point along the reaction coordinate.

The second change is the one that matters for inversion. A wavefunction does not stop dead at the edge of a valley. It leaks into the hill, fading away exponentially, and if the hill is thin enough and low enough, a little of it reaches the other side. So the molecule does not have to climb over the barrier to get to the other valley; there is a probability that it is simply found on the other side. This is quantum tunnelling.

One consequence is beautiful and is what you can actually measure. Because the two valleys are identical, the wavefunctions have to be either symmetric (the same on both sides, labelled +) or antisymmetric (equal and opposite, labelled −). Without tunnelling, those two would have exactly the same energy. With tunnelling, they are split apart by a small amount, the tunnelling splitting, ΔE, and each level becomes a closely spaced pair, a doublet. Friedrich Hund worked out this picture in 1927 , and it was soon used to explain a doubling of lines in ammonia’s spectrum . Ammonia’s ground-state splitting is about 0.79 cm⁻¹, which corresponds to a microwave frequency of about 23.8 GHz, and that is where the opening line of this post comes from. One of ammonia’s inversion lines near that frequency is also what the first maser, built in 1954, ran on .

The splitting is extraordinarily sensitive to the shape of the barrier. Make the hill a little higher or a little wider, and the splitting does not shrink a little; it shrinks exponentially. That sensitivity is why inversion is a good probe of the environment a molecule is in, and it is the thing to play with below.

There is another way to see what a splitting means. If you could prepare the molecule so that it is definitely in the left valley, that state is not one of the energy levels but a mixture of the + and − states of a pair. Left alone, the mixture does not stay put: it sloshes into the right valley and back again, and the time it takes to go there and back is set by the splitting, T = h/ΔE. A large splitting means fast sloshing, and a tiny splitting means the molecule stays where it is for a very long time.

For hydronium the back-and-forth takes well under a picosecond. For phosphine, which has a much higher and wider barrier, the calculated splitting is so small (well below 10⁻¹¹ cm⁻¹, with estimates that span several orders of magnitude depending on how you compute it) that the round trip would take anywhere from seconds to about a day, which on a molecular timescale is close to forever. No inversion splitting has ever been seen in phosphine’s spectrum, although full-dimensional calculations suggest it might show up in highly excited bending states . One of our molecules is a fast tunneller, and the other is, for practical purposes, stuck.

A cage made of carbon

C₆₀ is a hollow ball of sixty carbon atoms, arranged in twelve pentagons and twenty hexagons exactly like the panels of a football. It was discovered in 1985 , and its carbon atoms sit on a sphere about 7 Å across. Once you allow for the size of the carbon atoms’ own electron clouds, that leaves an empty space in the middle only about 3.7 Å across, and chemists have found ways to put things in it. Molecules inside fullerenes are called endohedral, and they are written with an @ sign, so a water molecule inside C₆₀ is H₂O@C₆₀.

This is not only a thought experiment. Using a sequence of reactions sometimes called “molecular surgery”, chemists open a hole in the cage, slip a molecule in, and stitch the hole closed again. Hydrogen was put inside C₆₀ in 2005 , and water in 2011 , and once inside, these molecules keep rotating and vibrating while held away from everything else. They have become a kind of laboratory for studying a single molecule in a very clean, very small box.

That raises a question. If tunnelling is so sensitive to the shape of the barrier, what does the box do to it? Physicists have known since the 1940s that squeezing a quantum oscillator into a box pushes its energy levels up , but a flip is a more delicate thing than a single vibration. Does squeezing a molecule make it harder to flip, because there is less room, or easier, because of how the cage pulls on it? Is the answer the same for a charged molecule and a neutral one? Our group had already looked at ammonia inside C₆₀ , and this paper asks the same questions for hydronium and phosphine.

To answer the question, though, we have to be able to calculate the double well for a molecule that is surrounded by sixty carbon atoms. That needs a detour into how molecules are calculated at all.

How do you calculate a molecule?

Everything about a molecule, its shape, its energy, how it vibrates, follows in principle from one equation, the Schrödinger equation. The trouble is in the words “in principle”.

The first simplification is the Born–Oppenheimer approximation. Nuclei are thousands of times heavier than electrons, so the electrons rearrange themselves almost instantly whenever the nuclei move. That means you can freeze the nuclei in place, solve for the electrons, and get an energy for that one arrangement of nuclei. Do that for many arrangements and you get a potential energy surface, and the double well from the last section is a slice through exactly such a surface.

So the real problem is the electrons. The difficulty is that the electrons all repel each other, so the position of every electron depends on where all the others are, and the wavefunction that describes them is a function of all their coordinates at once: three coordinates per electron. Hydronium has 10 electrons, so its wavefunction is a function of 30 variables. C₆₀ has 360 electrons, so its wavefunction lives in 1,080 dimensions. You cannot store such a thing, let alone solve for it directly.

Every practical method in quantum chemistry is a way of approximating this problem, and they trade accuracy for cost in different ways. Two families matter for this paper.

Hartree–Fock, and what it leaves out

The oldest approach is the Hartree–Fock method. Instead of letting every electron respond to every other electron individually, it lets each electron move in the average field of all the others. Each electron then gets its own wavefunction, an orbital, and these are the orbitals you meet in chemistry classes. The catch is that the average field depends on the orbitals, and the orbitals depend on the field, so the method goes round in a loop: guess the orbitals, build the field, solve for new orbitals, rebuild the field, and repeat until nothing changes. This loop is called the self-consistent field (SCF), and DFT uses the same idea.

What Hartree–Fock leaves out is that real electrons dodge each other on the fly, rather than just feeling an average. The energy that comes from this dodging is called the correlation energy. It is a small fraction of the total, but chemistry happens in small differences, so it matters a great deal. One part of it matters for this paper in particular: dispersion, the weak, attractive “London” force that arises when the electrons of two neighbouring molecules fluctuate in step with each other. It is why noble gases liquefy at all, and Hartree–Fock does not capture it.

Methods that add correlation back on top of Hartree–Fock are called post-Hartree–Fock, or wavefunction methods. MP2 adds it as a correction using perturbation theory. Coupled cluster goes much further, and the version known as CCSD(T), which treats single and double excitations of electrons in full and adds triple excitations approximately , is often called the “gold standard” of quantum chemistry, because for well-behaved molecules, with a large enough basis set, it gets energies right to within about a kilocalorie per mole. It is also very expensive. Its cost grows roughly as the seventh power of the size of the system, so doubling the size of a molecule makes the calculation about a hundred and twenty-eight times slower.

Density functional theory

Density functional theory (DFT) comes at the problem from the other end. In 1964, Hohenberg and Kohn proved something surprising: the ground-state energy of a system of electrons is completely determined by its electron density, the three-dimensional function from the figure above . You do not need the 3N-dimensional wavefunction to get the energy, in principle; the density, which is a function of only three coordinates, contains all the information.

The catch is that the proof says such a mapping from density to energy, a functional, exists, but not what it is. A year later, Kohn and Sham found a practical route . They imagined a fictitious system of electrons that do not interact with each other but have exactly the same density as the real ones. For non-interacting electrons, almost everything can be calculated exactly, and all the difficult many-body physics gets pushed into one leftover term, the exchange–correlation functional. If we knew that term exactly, DFT would be exact. We do not, and the hundreds of “functionals” in use today, with names like B3LYP, PBE0, M06-2X and B97-D, are different approximations to it. John Perdew described them as rungs on a “Jacob’s ladder”, climbing from simple approximations towards the exact answer, with each rung costing more .

In practice, DFT costs roughly as much as Hartree–Fock but includes a good part of the correlation energy, which is why it is the workhorse of computational chemistry. Its weaknesses are that there is no systematic way to improve a given functional, so you have to check how a functional does on problems similar to yours, and that most functionals miss dispersion too. They include correlation, but they work out the energy by looking at the density at and near each point, while dispersion is a correlation between electrons that are far apart. Stefan Grimme’s fix was pragmatic: add a simple, empirically fitted attraction between every pair of atoms that falls off as 1/r⁶, the way dispersion does. The functional we ended up using, B97-D, is one he built with that correction included from the start . For a molecule sitting inside a cage of sixty carbon atoms, dispersion is not a detail.

Basis sets

There is one more ingredient, and it is the part of a method’s name after the slash. Orbitals are smooth functions in three-dimensional space, and computers work with finite lists of numbers, so every calculation builds its orbitals out of a fixed set of simpler functions, a basis set, and only solves for how much of each to mix in.

The natural building blocks would be functions shaped like the orbitals of the hydrogen atom, which fall off as e−r. But the integrals that a calculation needs are much faster to compute with Gaussian functions, e−αr², so almost all of quantum chemistry builds its orbitals out of sums of Gaussians. A single Gaussian is the wrong shape: it is too flat at the nucleus, where the true orbital has a sharp point, and it dies away too quickly far from it. Add a few with different widths, and the sum gets close.

The basis sets in this paper have names like aug-cc-pVTZ, which unpacks as follows. cc-p means “correlation-consistent polarised”, a family designed by Thom Dunning so that results improve systematically as the sets get bigger . VTZ means valence triple-zeta: each valence orbital gets three functions of different sizes, so it can stretch or contract as the bonding demands. Polarised means there are also functions of higher angular momentum (d and f functions on a carbon atom, say) so that orbitals can bend and distort. And aug means augmented with very spread-out “diffuse” functions , which matter for ions, for weak interactions, and for the outer, loosely held parts of a molecule’s electron cloud. QZ, quadruple-zeta, is the next size up, and the larger the basis, the closer you get to the answer the method would give with a perfect basis, and the more it costs.

Why the cage needed DFT

Now the practical problem is clear. A free hydronium ion in the aug-cc-pVTZ basis is a small calculation. Put it inside C₆₀ and you add sixty carbon atoms, each of which brings its own set of basis functions, and the calculation is more than twenty times bigger. For a method whose cost grows as the seventh power of size, that is not a factor of twenty; it is a factor of billions.

So the paper splits the work. For the free molecules, which are small, we computed CCSD(T) energies with the large aug-cc-pVQZ basis along the path, to get the most reliable double wells we could, and compared them against experiment where experiment exists. For the caged molecules, the full double well had to come from DFT. And to check the strength of the binding between the guest and the cage, which needs high accuracy at only a few geometries, we used DLPNO-CCSD(T), a local approximation to coupled cluster developed in Frank Neese’s group, the group behind ORCA , which makes coupled-cluster-quality energies possible for systems the size of a fullerene.

Choosing a functional

Which DFT functional, then? This is where one has to be careful. In our earlier work on ammonia inside C₆₀, the M06-2X functional had reproduced the experimental inversion barrier well , so the obvious move would have been to use it again. Before trusting any functional, though, we tested several of them against CCSD(T)/aug-cc-pVQZ on the free molecules, where both could be computed.

For phosphine, almost everything gets the barrier right to within about 7%. For hydronium, the methods are all over the place, and M06-2X, which had done so well for ammonia, gives a barrier a third to a half too low, depending on the basis set. B97-D with the aug-cc-pVTZ basis was the only functional that did well on both molecules, so that is what we used for the caged systems. It is still about 13% low for hydronium (612 against 706 cm⁻¹), which is worth remembering when we get to the results.

I find this one of the more useful lessons of the project, and it is the one I would pass on to anyone starting out the way I did. There is no “best functional”. There is the functional that has been shown to work for your kind of problem, and the only way to know is to check.

Tracing the path

With the method chosen, the calculation of a double well goes through a few steps, each of which is a standard job type in a program like ORCA or Gaussian.

First, geometry optimisation: start from a rough guess of the pyramid and let the program move the atoms downhill until the forces on them vanish. That gives the bottom of a valley. Second, find the transition state, the flat geometry at the top of the hill. A transition state is a saddle point: it is a maximum along the reaction coordinate and a minimum in every other direction. A frequency calculation confirms which is which, because a true minimum has only real vibrational frequencies, while a transition state has exactly one imaginary frequency, whose motion is the flip itself. The barrier height is then just the difference in energy between the two.

Third, the path between them. An intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) calculation starts at the transition state and follows the steepest way down, in mass-weighted coordinates, until it reaches the valley . That gives one half of the double well, from the top of the hill to the bottom of the valley. But the wavefunctions also spread up the far wall of each valley, the side where the pyramid is squashed taller than its equilibrium shape, so we ran a second set of IRC calculations starting from compressed pyramids and following them back down. Joining the two pieces, and using the fact that the two valleys are mirror images, gives the whole double well.

Fourth, a smooth curve through the points. The paper fits each double well to the form

$$V(x) = A x^4 + B\, e^{-C x^2} - D x^2,$$

a quartic wall that keeps the molecule from flying apart, a Gaussian bump for the barrier in the middle, and an inverted parabola that deepens the two valleys. (The paper writes the last term as +Dx² with a negative D; I have written the minus sign explicitly.) The constant B is the energy of the flat geometry, x = 0, and it equals the barrier height when the valley floors sit at zero.

Finally, the quantum part. With V(x) in hand, the one-dimensional Schrödinger equation for motion along x can be solved numerically for the energy levels and wavefunctions, which the paper does with the shooting method. This is exactly the calculation the playground earlier in this post is running in your browser, and solving it with the fitted parameters from the paper reproduces the paper’s lowest energy levels to within a few cm⁻¹, and the higher ones to within about half a per cent.

What the cage does

Here is the main result, with the double wells for both molecules, free and caged.

Hydronium gets stuck. Inside C₆₀, the B97-D barrier for hydronium rises from 612 to about 870 cm⁻¹, and the two valleys move further apart (the pyramid gets about 25% taller). Both changes make tunnelling harder, and the ground-state splitting falls from 104 to 38 cm⁻¹, a drop by almost two-thirds. For the free molecule, B97-D overestimates the splitting compared with experiment (104 against 55 cm⁻¹), while CCSD(T) gets much closer (49 to 65 cm⁻¹ depending on the basis set), so the absolute numbers for the caged molecule should not be taken too literally. The direction and the rough size of the effect, though, are what DFT should get right, since the same method is being compared with itself.

Why would the valleys move apart? Our interpretation in the paper is that it comes from the charge. Hydronium is a cation, and its positive charge pulls the electron cloud of the cage towards it, inducing a negative charge on the inner surface of the C₆₀. The ion is then attracted to that induced charge, and the attraction favours the pyramidal shapes over the flat one. That is an interpretation, and the paper does not test it separately.

Phosphine barely notices. Phosphine’s barrier was already enormous, and confinement lowers it only slightly, from about 11,600 to 11,000 cm⁻¹. It is still more than fifty times room-temperature thermal energy, and tunnelling stays negligible inside the cage as well as outside it. What changes is mostly the shape of the well: phosphine’s umbrella vibration shifts up a little (1027 to 1056 cm⁻¹), and the spacing between its energy levels grows slightly, which is what you would expect if the walls of the well get a little steeper.

So the same cage does opposite things to the two molecules. For the charged, floppy hydronium, it raises the barrier and suppresses tunnelling. For the neutral, rigid phosphine, it lowers the barrier slightly and otherwise changes very little.

The geometries and vibrations tell the same story. In the cage, hydronium’s O–H bonds stretch slightly (0.982 to 0.995 Å) and their stretching vibrations shift to lower frequencies, while the bending motion that drives the flip stiffens (842 to 1037 cm⁻¹). Phosphine’s P–H bonds shorten (1.428 to 1.396 Å) and all of its vibrations shift to higher frequencies, which is what you would expect of a molecule that is being pressed on from all sides. The cage itself barely moves: the carbon atoms shift by only about 0.07 Å (root-mean-square) when a guest is inside.

What holds them in

Is the molecule actually stable in there, or would it rather be outside? The interaction energy answers that: the energy of the guest inside the cage, minus the energies of the guest and the empty cage apart. A negative number means the combination is more stable than the two pieces.

With DLPNO-CCSD(T), the interaction energy is −30.8 kcal/mol for hydronium and −13.4 kcal/mol for phosphine . Both are bound, hydronium much more strongly. (B97-D gets hydronium about right, but underestimates phosphine’s binding by nearly a factor of two, which is a useful caution about using one method for everything.)

To see why they are bound, we split the interaction energy into physically meaningful pieces using the local energy decomposition (LED) built into ORCA . It is easiest to read as a budget.

Both molecules have to pay a large up-front cost, the electronic preparation energy, to squeeze their electron clouds into the space and to let the cage’s electrons rearrange around them. That cost is larger for phosphine (174.5 against 117.8 kcal/mol), because phosphine is the bigger molecule. They pay it back in different ways. Hydronium is held mostly by electrostatics, which makes sense for a positive ion surrounded by a polarisable cloud of 360 electrons. Phosphine gets a larger share of its binding from dispersion (38.9 against 18.3 kcal/mol), along with exchange and polarisation, the soft, correlated attraction that Hartree–Fock misses and that Grimme’s correction was designed to add back.

What this does not tell us

It is worth being plain about the limits of all this.

The double wells are one-dimensional. A real molecule has many ways to vibrate, and the umbrella motion is coupled to the others; for free hydronium, full-dimensional calculations have shown that the coupling is strong . Reducing inversion to a single coordinate captures the essentials, but the numbers it gives are those of a model.

The caged results come from DFT, and the free-molecule benchmarks show that B97-D underestimates hydronium’s barrier and overestimates its splitting. The comparison between free and caged is made with the same method on both sides, which is why I trust the direction of the effects more than their exact sizes, but I cannot rule out that the method’s errors behave differently inside the cage.

The phosphine splittings are so small that they sit at the edge of what these calculations can resolve. The paper itself points out that the computed values depend strongly on the method and that tiny numerical noise along the flat part of the potential can be amplified when the curve is interpolated. I would read the phosphine splittings as “negligible, free or caged”, and not put weight on any single number.

And these are calculations on idealised systems. As far as I know, neither OH₃⁺@C₆₀ nor PH₃@C₆₀ has been made in a laboratory (even ammonia inside C₆₀ was still described as a target in 2022), and a real sample of a caged cation would also have a counter-ion somewhere nearby, which our model does not include. So the predictions here are waiting for an experiment, and I would be curious to see whether the suppression of hydronium’s tunnelling shows up in a spectrum.

Coming back to it

What I find satisfying about this problem is how much of physical chemistry it touches with so few atoms. A four-atom molecule and a ball of carbon are enough to bring in potential energy surfaces, tunnelling, the particle in a box, the whole ladder of electronic structure methods, and the question of where binding comes from, and the answers are not the same for two molecules that look almost alike on paper.

It also raises questions I do not know the answers to. If a charged guest polarises the cage and that changes its barrier, could you tune a guest’s tunnelling by changing the cage, say with a larger fullerene, or one with a metal atom or a different charge? Would an external electric field, pushing on the induced charge, do something similar? And how well does the one-dimensional picture hold up once the cage is allowed to vibrate along with the guest?

Behind every number in this post is a lot of practical work that does not appear in the paper: input files, convergence failures, jobs that ran for days on a cluster and then had to be run again. I learned most of that by doing it badly first, and I want to write it up properly, which is what the next post will be about.
